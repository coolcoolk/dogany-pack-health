# relationships-communication/roster-without-chore-list-021

A hundred and fifteen people in a table, each with a last-contact date, is two
products that look almost the same from outside.

One is a memory aid: it hands over a name and the last thing the two of them did
together, and gets out of the way. The other is a debt ledger: it tracks who is
owed a message and how late it is. The second one is what a contact tracker turns
into on its own, without anybody deciding to build it, and the owner has a
standing rule against every surface that behaves that way -- no lecturing, no
gatekeeping, no streak language, and when he is behind on something, adjust the
mechanism or renegotiate the target rather than push.

The useful part is not the principle. It is how the principle is made to survive
a model that has just found four genuinely interesting people to bring up.

It survives because the budget is not advice. It is a cap in the code.

At most two enrichment questions in a day, across everybody, not per person. At
most one proactive batch a week, holding two or three names. The casual check-in
for somebody with no contact record at all rides inside that same weekly cap
rather than getting a budget of its own -- which sounds like a footnote and is
not, because a second budget line is exactly how a cap quietly doubles. A
sixty-day window before the same person can be raised that way again. Anyone with
a meeting already in the calendar is dropped before the ranking even runs, since
suggesting you contact someone you are about to see is the surface at its most
transparently mechanical. And when he waves a suggestion off, it disappears and
is not raised again.

The reason to put those numbers in code rather than in an instruction is
mechanical. An instruction is a reminder, and a reminder is the control that
fails at the exact moment it matters -- the model that found four people will
surface four. Moving the number into the code takes the decision out of the moment
of temptation entirely. That is the same forcing-point logic the framework applies
to its own rules; nothing here was tested against a prose-only version, and the
argument is that it is structural rather than that it was measured.

Then there are the two exits, and a roster without exits becomes an obligation by
arithmetic alone, since names only ever go in.

Fade is permanent, offered once, gently, and framed as a perfectly good outcome:
some relationships are supposed to drift, and a system that cannot represent that
forces the owner either to lie to it or to feel bad. Snooze is a different thing
on purpose -- dated, reversible, with a recorded reason, for the friend who moved
abroad for two years. Collapsing them would permanently demote someone who is
simply away. Both need an explicit yes, and neither is ever inferred from a
passing remark, because guessing wrong means quietly removing a person from his
attention without being asked.

There is one hard bound on fade, and it comes from the evidence side of this
domain rather than from taste. Fade is offered when a gap runs past the owner's
own declared band, and for no other reason. Never because a relationship looks
one-sided. Never with any reading of whether the other person reciprocates. The
evidence row on tie reciprocity is unusually direct about this: nothing in that
literature links an asymmetric tie to any outcome at all, and an agent that told
someone which of their friendships were not returned would be inventing both the
measurement and the harm. With three hundred-odd stored relations sitting right
there, that is the most tempting feature on this surface, and it is the one the
evidence forbids outright rather than merely fails to support.

Last, and it is the rule that gets broken by inaction rather than by decision:
nothing accumulates. A suggestion that was not acted on does not roll over. There
is no queue of people owed a message, no count of missed weeks, no running total
anywhere. There is no rhythm to be behind on, and most of the empty space in the
log is unknown rather than genuinely empty, so a backlog would be a debt
assembled out of two things the system does not know. A list that is not
explicitly forbidden to grow will grow, and once it has, the debt ledger is built.

The numbers themselves -- two a day, two or three a week, sixty days -- were
chosen, not measured. They are an attention budget picked to be obviously
survivable, and they are adjustable the moment the owner says so. What is not
adjustable is that there is a cap and that it lives in the code. If some new
feature turns out to need its own budget line, that is not a reason to grant one.
It is the signal that a cap is being routed around.
