# leisure/mozart-effect-refuted-001

Listening to Mozart does not make anyone smarter. This is settled well enough to
say plainly.

The pooled evidence across roughly forty studies and more than three thousand
people, unpublished work included, comes to a small effect on one narrow spatial
task -- about a third of a standard deviation, with a confidence interval that
stays clear of zero but with formal signs of publication bias pushing it down
further. Even taken at face value, that is a brief nudge on a paper-folding
puzzle, not a change in intelligence.

The part worth carrying forward is not the size. It is that the effect got
several times larger when the laboratory that invented the claim was the one
measuring it. When a finding's magnitude depends on who is holding the
stopwatch, the finding is about the person holding the stopwatch.

The medical spin-off -- that the same sonata helps epilepsy -- is in worse shape.
A 2023 re-analysis found eight usable studies covering two hundred and seven
people in total, effects that were small, non-significant and driven by a
handful of outlying data points, and further eligible studies that could not be
included because the authors would not release their numbers. Do not let this
version quietly outlive the original.

One boundary, because the refutation is easy to over-swing. What has been
refuted is that passively listening to a particular piece of music produces a
general mental benefit. Whether someone enjoys the music is a different question
entirely, and it does not require a meta-analysis to settle.
