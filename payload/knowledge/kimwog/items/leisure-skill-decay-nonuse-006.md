# leisure/skill-decay-nonuse-006

Skills go away when you stop using them, and the pooled evidence puts a size on
it. Across a hundred and eighty-nine data points from fifty-three studies, the
loss runs from essentially nothing immediately after training to well over a full
standard deviation after more than a year of not touching the thing.

The more useful half of the result is which skills go first. Physical, natural
and speed-based tasks hold up markedly better than cognitive, artificial and
accuracy-based ones. Translated into an actual hobby: after a year away from an
instrument, the hands remember more than the head does. The physical motion
survives. What goes is the repertoire, the memorised material, the fine
intonation, the precision at the top of the range -- everything that was
carried cognitively rather than motorically.

That is also why coming back feels the way it does. It is not uniform rustiness.
It is one system mostly intact sitting on top of another that has emptied out,
and the mismatch is what makes the return unpleasant enough that people quit
twice.

Two limits, stated because this item would otherwise be over-read. The studies
pooled here are occupational and training research, not people with hobbies, and
the pooling is from 1998 with nothing newer found. So the direction and the
pattern of which-decays-faster travel. The number does not. Nobody should be told
how much of their playing is left after a year off on the strength of this.

What it does license is a claim about structure. The driver of loss is the length
of the gap, not the total hours ever accumulated. Two hundred hours of playing
spread thin does not protect against six months of silence. Short and frequent
beats rare and long, for keeping a thing rather than for building it -- which
happens to be exactly what the practice literature concludes from the other
direction.
