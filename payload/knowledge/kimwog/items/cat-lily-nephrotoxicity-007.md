# cat/lily-nephrotoxicity-007

Most household plant warnings are a genre rather than a literature. This one is
different: someone did the experiment.

An experimental study of Easter lily found that aqueous extracts of the leaves
and the flowers were toxic to the kidney and the pancreas in cats, and went
further, narrowing most of the toxic activity down to a single fraction of the
floral extract. The clinical picture included vomiting, depression, marked
thirst and urination, and the laboratory signature of kidney injury. On
pathology, acute necrosis of the proximal convoluted tubules.

That lesion is the whole point. Most plant warnings describe irritation or an
upset stomach. This is renal injury, demonstrated, which is why a lily exposure
in a cat household is a phone call to a veterinarian rather than something to
keep an eye on.

Three things are left out on purpose, and they are the three things everyone
repeats.

No amount. The circulating advice attaches quantities -- a petal, pollen groomed
off the coat, water from the vase. Some of that is probably right. None of it was
in the record read here, which reports extract fractions rather than a
household-scale dose, and inventing a threshold at the moment of answering is
exactly the failure mode this warehouse is built against.

No timeline. Onset windows circulate widely and none was in the source.

And no genus. The paper's own finding is that only certain extracts of one plant
were toxic, which is a restriction it discovered rather than a licence to
generalise. The broader claim that every lily and every daylily is dangerous to
cats may well be true; it is not established by this study, and it would need its
own source.

The useful consequence is preventive rather than reactive, and it is unusual in
this domain for that reason. This hazard walks into a house as a bouquet, from
someone who was not thinking about the cat. The moment where an assistant can
actually help is when flowers are being ordered or have just arrived -- not when
a cat is already unwell. What happens after an exposure is a veterinarian's
business, and this item says only that it is one.
