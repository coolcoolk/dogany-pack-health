# learning-habits/21-day-myth-refuted-003

The popular "21 days to form a habit" claim has no empirical basis -- it
traces to a 1960 anecdotal claim by Maxwell Maltz, not a study. The studies
that actually measured it report medians of 59 and 66 days, means of 106 and
154 days in one small study, a mean of 91 days in a fourth, and a very wide
individual range (4 to 335 days).
These are separate study figures that a 2024 review lists side by side, not a
pooled meta-analytic estimate, and the two medians describe only the
participants whose automaticity curves could be fitted.

Refute the 21-day figure confidently. Carry the specific day-count numbers
with a caveat: only 4 of the 20 underlying studies measured duration directly,
and the rest rely on self-report scales, so the precise numbers are somewhat
less certain than the myth-refutation itself. The medians also describe only
the people who got there: in one of the four studies just 23% of participants
reached the habit threshold at all. The often-quoted 66 days is a plateau
point, not a finish line: it is when automaticity stopped rising for the
typical person whose curve fitted, in a 12-week study, so anything much past
84 days is a projection of the curve rather than an observation.
