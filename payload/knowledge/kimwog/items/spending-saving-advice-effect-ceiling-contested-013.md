# spending-saving/advice-effect-ceiling-contested-013

Does financial advice change financial behaviour? Two meta-analyses in leading
journals give opposite-sounding answers, and both are honest.

The 2014 review pooled 168 papers and concluded that efforts to improve
financial literacy explain about a tenth of one percent of the variance in what
people actually do; that whatever effect exists decays, with even lengthy
courses showing negligible behavioural traces after twenty months; and that the
much stronger correlations in non-experimental work are probably omitted
variables. Its recommendation was to stop trying to educate in general and start
intervening at the moment of a specific decision.

The 2022 review restricted itself to 76 randomised experiments covering more
than 160,000 people and found real positive effects on both knowledge and
behaviour, comparable in size to education interventions in other fields, and
holding up under publication-bias correction, under a top-journals-only
restriction, and under a power restriction. Its average effect across outcomes
sits around a tenth of a standard deviation.

They are not looking at the same evidence. The earlier review had 13 randomised
trials available; by the later one there were 76, almost all of them run after
the first review appeared. The later authors say their estimate is at least three
times the earlier weighted average, and more than five times once cross-study
heterogeneity is modelled, and they attribute the difference to exactly that
growth. Anyone citing one of these papers without the other is presenting a live
methodological dispute as a settled fact.

What both agree on is the ceiling, and the ceiling is the useful part. Effects,
where they exist, are fractions of a standard deviation. They fade with distance
from delivery. And the form that survives the harshest critique is guidance
attached to a live decision rather than instruction delivered in general. One
further caution on the optimistic estimate: nearly three quarters of its effects
come from below-median-income samples and about three fifths from developing
economies, which is not the population a Korean household ledger serves.

For anything built on giving advice, this is the honest self-assessment: the
lever is real, it is small, it decays, and it works best pressed at the moment of
the decision rather than in a summary afterwards.
