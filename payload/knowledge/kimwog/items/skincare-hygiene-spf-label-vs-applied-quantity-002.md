# skincare-hygiene/spf-label-vs-applied-quantity-002

The number on the bottle is a laboratory measurement taken at a thickness nobody
applies.

SPF is defined as a dose ratio: the ultraviolet dose needed to just redden
protected skin, divided by the dose needed to just redden unprotected skin. It
is measured with the product spread at 2 mg per square centimetre. It is not a
time multiplier, and the belief that SPF 30 means thirty times longer outdoors is
a folk model that the definition does not contain.

Investigations of how much people actually apply under natural conditions land
between roughly 0.39 and 1.0 mg per square centimetre. Somewhere between a fifth
and a half of the test thickness.

The penalty for that is not proportional. In an in vivo phototest, twenty
volunteers had five marked areas on the back treated with an SPF 4 product at
0.5, 1, 2 and 4 mg per square centimetre and then phototested. The protection
delivered rose with quantity in a shape best fitted by exponential growth,
r-squared 0.903, and the authors read it as the effective SPF falling roughly to
the square root of the labelled figure at half the test thickness and to the
fourth root at a quarter of it. Read that as the shape of the penalty, not as a
calculator. It was one small study using a low-SPF preparation, and the products
people buy are nothing like SPF 4.

Two other failure modes come out of the same literature and are cheaper to fix
than either of the above: areas simply missed during application, and ultraviolet
exposure that happens before the product goes on at all.

The reviewers' own conclusion is worth carrying as written. Current labelling
overrates what a given product delivers once real use is accounted for, and may
mislead people into feeling safe extending their time in the sun. Their proposed
fix is not "educate everyone to apply 2 mg per square centimetre", which they
regard as unrealistic, but a two-step instruction: apply before exposure, and
reapply once within the first hour.

One thing deliberately not shipped here. The familiar "reapply every two hours"
is a labelling convention, and no primary measurement supporting that specific
interval was located in this pass. It is not asserted in this item, and if it is
ever spoken it must be attributed to the label, not to evidence.
