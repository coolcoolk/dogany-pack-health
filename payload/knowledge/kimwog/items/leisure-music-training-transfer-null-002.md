# leisure/music-training-transfer-null-002

Learning an instrument does not make a child better at anything except music.

The evidence for this is unusually clean. A meta-analysis pooling two hundred
and fifty-four effect sizes from fifty-four studies across nearly seven thousand
children did not simply average the field. It sorted the field by how well each
study was built. In the studies that randomly assigned children and compared
them against an equally engaging non-musical activity, the effect of music
training on cognitive and academic outcomes is zero, and remarkably consistent
in being zero. The positive results live entirely in the studies that did
neither -- no random assignment, and a control group that sat doing nothing.

That distinction matters more than the average does. It means the field does not
contain a disagreement to be settled; it contains a design flaw with a
predictable signature.

The three usual rescues were tested and all three failed. It does not work
better for younger children. It does not work better with longer training. It
does not work better for one kind of outcome than another -- verbal, non-verbal,
speed, all null. And the entirely separate correlational literature, which is
free of the training-study design problems, points the same way.

Now the boundary, which is the reason to keep this item rather than merely to
remember the headline. Everything above is about transfer to things that are not
music. Practice absolutely does make people better at the instrument; that is
not in question and is covered elsewhere in this domain.

The practical consequence for anyone talking to a person about their hobby: do
not sell the hobby on brain benefits. There aren't any, the person didn't ask
for any, and the justification is one this evidence removes.
