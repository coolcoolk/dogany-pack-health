# work-career/burnout-measurement-incoherence-009

Any burn-out percentage you meet is a statement about a cutoff, not a
measurement of a workforce.

A systematic review pulled prevalence data from 182 studies covering 109,628
people across 45 countries. Almost all of them used a version of the same
questionnaire. Between them they used at least 142 different definitions of
what counted as burned out. Among the studies using the common questionnaire
alone, there were at least 47 distinct definitions of overall prevalence.

Reported prevalence ranged from 0 percent to 80.5 percent.

The reviewers had planned to pool the studies into a single estimate. They
gave up, and said so: the variation in design and in how burn-out was
ascertained, plus the statistical heterogeneity, made pooling inappropriate.

So the operating rule is simple. A burn-out figure without its definition
attached carries no information. Two figures with different cutoffs are not
comparable, no matter how confidently they are placed side by side. And a
headline that a given profession is more burned out than another can be
nothing more than a comparison of thresholds; check before believing it.

The obvious limit: this review is about physicians. The spread of definitions
is a property of the instrument and the convention around it rather than of
medicine, so the caution travels; the specific numbers do not.
