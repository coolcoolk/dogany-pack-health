# leisure/fiction-social-cognition-small-003

The claim that reading fiction makes you better at understanding other people is
partly true, in a much smaller way than it is usually told, and probably not in
the direction it is usually told.

The famous experiment does not hold. In 2013 a study reported that reading a
short passage of literary fiction -- specifically literary, not popular --
immediately improved performance on a test of reading emotional states from
photographs of people's eyes. Three independent research teams then tried it
again with seven hundred and ninety-two participants across four conditions.
Literary fiction beat nothing. Not popular fiction, not non-fiction, not sitting
there reading nothing at all.

But the wider literature is not empty. Pooling fifty-three effects from fourteen
experiments, fiction reading does produce a small improvement on these
social-cognition tasks -- around a seventh of a standard deviation, and it
survives the checks for publication bias. Small and real in a laboratory. Not
debunked, and not what the headline said either.

The most interesting result is the one that keeps appearing in both papers and
that neither set of authors treats as an intervention effect. How much fiction
someone has read across their whole life predicts how well they do on the test,
in every condition, including the group that read nothing that day. The
replication team's own reading is that people who are good at reading others
gravitate toward fiction, or that decades of reading slowly builds the skill, or
both, with plain verbal ability possibly doing some of the work. That is a
lifelong trait correlation, and turning it into a twenty-minute intervention is
the mistake the original study made.

Finally, the ceiling on all of it. Every outcome in this literature is a score on
a test of photographs of eyes. Not one of these studies observed anyone treating
another person better. The meta-analysts say so themselves when they recommend
that someone check whether the effect transfers to real social functioning --
which is a polite way of recording that nobody has.
