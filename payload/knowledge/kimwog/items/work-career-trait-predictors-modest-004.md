# work-career/trait-predictors-modest-004

Stable personality traits do predict work outcomes. They predict them weakly,
and one heavily promoted trait mostly re-measures another one.

Conscientiousness is the strongest of the Big Five for work, and it is the
trait careers advice leans on most. Its operational validity is .19 as a
general measure and .25 when the questionnaire is written to be about work
specifically. For comparison, a structured interview sits at .42. Being a
conscientious person is real and it helps; it is not a large lever.

Grit is the harder case, because it was sold as a large lever. The meta-
analysis pooled 584 effect sizes from 88 samples covering 66,807 people and
reported three things: the two-facet structure that defines grit is not
confirmed by the data; grit correlates only moderately with performance and
with staying in a role; and grit is very strongly correlated with
conscientiousness, which raises the question of whether it is a distinct
construct at all.

The authors' own summary is that interventions to increase grit may have only
weak effects, and that the useful residue is the perseverance-of-effort facet,
which does add something beyond conscientiousness for academic performance.

Two things to hold at once, which is why this is flagged as unsettled. The
perseverance facet is not nothing. And the popular version -- that sustained
passion for very long-term goals is the thing that separates outcomes -- is
not what the pooled evidence shows.

The useful reframe for someone planning a career: trait-level self-description
is a poor place to look for leverage, because the measured effects are small
and the traits are hard to move. Structure, environment and specific
behaviour carry more of the variance that anyone can actually act on.
