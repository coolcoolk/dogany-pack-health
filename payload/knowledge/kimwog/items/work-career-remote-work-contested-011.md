# work-career/remote-work-contested-011

There is no general fact about whether remote work makes people more or less
productive. There are four good studies of four different arrangements, and
they point different ways.

The famous one. A randomised trial at a Chinese travel agency, call-centre
staff who volunteered, nine months: performance rose 13 percent -- 9 points of
that from working more minutes per shift, 4 points from more calls per minute.
Attrition halved. And their promotion rate, holding performance constant,
FELL. When the firm later let everyone choose, over half the experimental
group switched, and the measured gain nearly doubled to 22 percent, which the
authors put down to selection and learning, not a bigger treatment effect.

The recent one. A randomised trial with 1,612 graduate-level engineers,
marketing and finance staff: hybrid working cut attrition by 33 percent and
raised satisfaction, without damaging performance.

The selection one. Before offices closed, remote workers answered 12 percent
fewer calls per hour. After offices closed, the gap narrowed by 4 points, and
formerly on-site workers saw call quality and promotion rates fall. With
everybody remote, an 8 percent gap remained -- so most of the original gap was
about who takes remote jobs, not about the arrangement.

The forced one. Over 10,000 skilled professionals pushed fully remote by the
pandemic: hours worked rose, output slipped slightly, so productivity fell
by 8 to 19 percent. Coordination time and meetings went up,
uninterrupted work time went down.

The pattern that reconciles them: voluntary and hybrid arrangements in jobs
with clear output look fine or good. Forced full-time remote work in
coordination-heavy knowledge work looks bad. And a chunk of the raw difference
between remote and on-site workers is about who chooses which.

Two things worth refusing when they come up. The 13 percent figure applied to
salaried knowledge workers under a mandate -- wrong population, wrong
arrangement. And the same figure quoted without the promotion penalty that
appeared in the very same trial.
