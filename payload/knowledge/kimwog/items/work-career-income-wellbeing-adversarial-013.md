# work-career/income-wellbeing-adversarial-013

The line that happiness stops improving above a certain salary is not what the
evidence says, and the correction came from the people who published the
original claim.

The story in three moves. A 2010 study found day-to-day emotional well-being
rising with income up to a point and then flattening. A 2021 study using a
different, finer measurement found no flattening at all. Rather than trade
papers, the two sides ran a joint reanalysis and published the result in 2023.

What they found: the flattening is real, but only for the least happy people.
For everyone else, well-being keeps rising with log income, and in the
happiest group it accelerates.

They also explain both errors, which is the rare part. The 2010 study's
measure hit a ceiling and could not tell degrees of happiness apart -- it would
have been right if it had been described as a finding about UNHAPPINESS. And
neither side expected that more income changes the SHAPE of the well-being
distribution rather than just shifting its middle.

For a job decision, the usable direction is this. More money does keep
associating with better days for most people, so the plateau should not be
used as a reason to stop caring about pay. But among the least happy, the
relationship rises only up to a threshold and then goes flat -- which is the
evidence-backed version of the intuition that past a point you cannot outearn a
bad situation. (It compares people at different income levels; it does not
track one person's change after a raise.)

Limits: these are United States self-report samples and the relationship is
correlational throughout. It says what tends to go together, not what causes
what.
