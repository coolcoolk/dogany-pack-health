# home/otc-foggers-bed-bugs-007

Bug bombs do not work on bed bugs. This is one of the few household folk
remedies where somebody actually ran the test.

Three commercially available consumer total-release foggers were evaluated
against five different bed bug populations. The result was little if any adverse
effect on the insects. The authors described it as the first scientific evidence
that these products should not be recommended for bed bug control.

The reason is worth carrying, because it generalises further than the result
does. A fogger releases insecticide that settles on exposed surfaces. Bed bugs
live in cracks, seams, mattress piping and structural harbourages. The delivery
mechanism and the target's behaviour do not meet. Nothing about a bigger can or
a longer discharge fixes that mismatch.

So the practical instruction is not "foggers are weak, use two." It is that this
is a purchase with a measured null attached, and its real cost is the weeks it
buys the infestation while somebody waits to see if it worked.

Three limits, all of them real.

This is bed bugs. Cockroaches, ants, and everything else were not tested here
and were not researched in this pass. The null does not travel, and stretching
it would be the same error this warehouse refuses elsewhere.

This item does not tell you what to do instead. It establishes that one product
class fails at one job. A verified alternative protocol was not researched to
primary level here, so none is asserted. Refusing the thing that does not work
is the whole finding.

And the study is from 2012, testing products of that vintage. The behavioural
explanation for the failure is not product-specific and does not go stale, but a
claim about a particular item on a shelf today would need its own check.
