# home/sponge-sanitation-folklore-011

There are two confident claims about kitchen sponges in circulation. One is
folklore. The other is a viral correction of the folklore. Neither survives
reading the papers.

Start with what is actually measured, because that part is solid. Used kitchen
sponges are colonised at extraordinary density -- image analysis put local
concentrations as high as 5.4 x 10^10 cells per cubic centimetre, distributed
throughout the sponge tissue and concentrated in internal cavities where
biofilm-like structures had formed. Five of the ten most abundant taxa were
closely related to risk-group-2 species. A used sponge is not a bit dirty. It is
a structured microbial habitat.

Now the folklore: microwave it, or boil it, and it is clean again.

And the correction that went viral: no, sanitising your sponge selects for the
nastier organisms and makes it worse.

The correction traces to an observational finding in the 2017 paper -- two of
the ten dominant taxa appeared in significantly greater proportions in sponges
whose users said they cleaned them regularly. The authors themselves phrased
this as questioning such methods over the long term, which is careful. The
problem is that nobody assigned the cleaning. Users decided. Which means the
sponges being cleaned may simply be the sponges that provoked cleaning. That is
confounding by indication, and it is fatal to a causal reading.

The follow-up study fixed exactly that. Twenty participants got new sponges and
used them under normal household conditions for four weeks; ten of them
sanitised on a standardised microwave protocol, ten did not. The microwaved
sponges showed altered community composition and metabolic properties, with
structural diversity down and functional diversity up. And then the authors
wrote, in the abstract, that follow-up studies will have to show whether these
changes are more positive or negative.

That sentence is the whole answer. A shift in which organisms are present, with
no measured link to illness, contamination or food safety, is not a hygiene
result in either direction.

So both claims are refused. Microwaving your sponge has no demonstrated benefit.
It also has no demonstrated harm. What the evidence supports is much duller: a
sponge is a habitat, and the reliable move is to stop treating it as something
you can clean.

Two boundaries. A metagenomic composition shift is not a health endpoint, and
converting one into a risk statement is how this topic went wrong in the first
place. And cross-contamination to food, cutting-board material, and refrigerator
temperature are food-safety questions belonging to the cooking domain's own
lane; this item deliberately stays off them.
