# task-management/switch-cost-not-a-productivity-percentage-006

"Research shows multitasking costs up to 40% of your productive time" is
attributed, essentially always, to a single 2001 laboratory paper on task
switching. That paper was read in full for this item. It does not contain the
figure, or any percentage of productive time, or any measurement of a workday.

What it does contain: four experiments in which people alternated between
classifying shapes under different rules, or solving arithmetic problems under
different operations. The switching costs it measured are in milliseconds --
from under a tenth of a second in the easiest condition to around a second in
the hardest. They grew with rule complexity and shrank when participants were
cued about which task came next.

That is a genuine and well-characterised finding about the cost of reloading a
task rule. It is not a productivity statistic, and it cannot be converted into
one: the experiments contain no notion of a productive day against which a
percentage could be taken.

Note carefully what is NOT being claimed here. Switching is not free, and the
paper is not being debunked. What is being refused is a specific numerical
claim that was never in it. The percentage appears to originate in a remark by
one of the authors in press material about the study rather than in the study.
The press page could not be retrieved directly in this pass, so this item
asserts only the part that was verified by reading the paper: the number is not
in the research.

If an interruption or switching cost needs to be spoken about, use the two
items that actually measured something --
task-management/interruption-resumption-measured-004 for elapsed time and
resumption in real offices, and
task-management/momentary-interruption-error-cost-005 for the error cost of
very short interruptions.
