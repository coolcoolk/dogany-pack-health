# work-career/self-employment-earnings-split-016

Does working for yourself pay more or less than working for someone else? The
two best answers disagree, and they disagree about who counts.

The pessimistic result. Using United States panel data, most people who go
self-employed have both lower starting earnings and lower earnings growth than
they would have had in paid work -- a median gap of about 35 percent for
someone ten years into their own business. The author's reading is not that
these people are irrational; it is that the non-financial benefits must be
substantial, because they stay anyway.

The optimistic result. A later paper splits the self-employed into those who
incorporated and those who did not, and argues these are two different
populations. The incorporated do non-routine cognitive work; the
unincorporated do manual work. And on earnings it reports the opposite sign:
becoming an entrepreneur is associated with a material earnings increase, at
every decile of the distribution.

Neither settles it, because the disagreement is not about data quality. It is
about who belongs in the category and how to handle the fact that people are
almost never randomly assigned to start a business.

What survives both, and is the actually useful part:

An average return to self-employment is not a meaningful number on its own.
The population is too heterogeneous. Anyone quoting one has chosen a
definition, and the definition largely decides the answer.

And the traits that predict entry into the higher-earning kind were
measurable in adolescence -- education, learning aptitude, self-esteem, and
notably a greater propensity for rule-breaking. That is uncomfortable for
advice that treats the decision as equally available to everyone.

The Korean case is not covered by either paper, and the gap matters here more
than usual, because a large share of Korean self-employment is entered after
being pushed out of a main job rather than chosen against a paid alternative.
