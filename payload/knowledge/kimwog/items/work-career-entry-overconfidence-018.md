# work-career/entry-overconfidence-018

People starting a business rate their own odds well above the odds they give
businesses like theirs, and the size of that split has been measured.

Nearly 3,000 people who had recently become business owners were asked two
questions.

What are your own odds of success? Mean answer: 8.1 out of 10. Eighty-one
percent said 7 out of 10 or better. Thirty-three percent said 10 out of 10 --
dead certain.

What are the odds for any business like yours? Mean answer: 5.9 out of 10.
Only 39 percent said 7 out of 10 or better.

Asked to compare directly, 68 percent said their own odds were better than
others in the same business. Five percent said worse.

The gap between 8.1 and 5.9 is the whole result. It is a systematic exemption
of oneself from odds that are otherwise accepted -- and even the 5.9 for others
is rosier than the under-half five-year survival the same paper reviews.

The practical consequence, and it is not the obvious one: showing someone the
survival base rate may not change their forecast much, because they already
place themselves above whatever they believe about other people (an inference
from the survey, not a tested intervention). What might change it is asking them
to name specifically what makes their case different, and then asking how many
of the people in the base rate could have said the same sentence.

Limits, stated plainly: this is one survey, from 1985, in the United States,
built on self-report. Its strength is that each person served as their own
comparison, which is why the finding travels better than an absolute optimism
score would.
