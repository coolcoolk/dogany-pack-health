  - note: >
      The core experimental result. Across a series of preregistered experiments,
      Liu et al. 2023 document a ROBUST UNDERESTIMATION of how much other people
      appreciate being reached out to. The proposed mechanism is that responders,
      unlike initiators, are focused on their own feeling of SURPRISE at being
      contacted, and a focus on surprise predicts greater appreciation. The
      moderators follow from that mechanism and are the operationally useful
      part: the underestimation is MAGNIFIED when the contact is more surprising
      -- in a surprising rather than unsurprising context, and between more
      socially DISTANT rather than socially close others.

      appreciation is LARGEST for the people you are least close to. The instinct

      the case where the forecast is most wrong. This does not say distant
      contacts matter more. It says the reluctance is most miscalibrated exactly
      where it feels most justified.

# relationships-communication/reach-out-appreciation-gap-013

Most of this domain is people filling in questionnaires about their
relationships. This row is different: it is experiments, with random assignment,
comparing what people PREDICTED would happen against what actually happened. That
is why it carries more weight than its size suggests.

The finding is a forecasting error. People consistently underestimate how much
others appreciate being contacted. The proposed reason is that the person
receiving the message is focused on being pleasantly surprised, and the person
sending it is not thinking about surprise at all -- so the sender models the
wrong thing.

The moderator is the part worth remembering. The gap between expected and actual
appreciation is largest when the contact is more surprising, and largest between
people who are less close. The instinct says messaging someone you have not
spoken to in a long time is the awkward case. The measurement says that is the
case where your forecast is most wrong.

An independent line of experiments finds the same misforecast in a completely
different setting. Commuters on trains and buses were randomly instructed either
to talk to a nearby stranger or not to. The ones who talked reported a better
experience, and no less productive a one. A separate group asked to predict the
outcome expected the opposite -- they thought solitude would be more pleasant.
And in a waiting-room study, the people who were talked to did just as well as
the people instructed to talk, so the initiator is not imposing a cost.

There is a weaker supporting point about peripheral contacts. People reported
more happiness and more belonging on days when they interacted with more
acquaintances than usual. That is diary correlation rather than experiment, and a
good day could just as easily be producing the interaction, so it is carried as a
reason not to dismiss acquaintance-level contact rather than as a demonstrated
effect.

Now the limit, which is as important as the finding. Not one of these studies
prescribes how often to reach out. They all measure a single act: what you
expected, and what happened. There is no interval in them, no target, no
schedule. Turning "people appreciate it more than you expect" into "contact
everyone every three weeks" invents a quantity that no study here contains, and
manufactures an obligation out of a correction to a forecast.

The supported form is narrow and it is the right shape for the moment it applies
to. When someone hesitates over whether to send the message, the evidence says:
you are probably underestimating how welcome it is, and most of all for the
person you are least close to.
