# leisure/vacation-recovery-fadeout-007

Time off helps and the help evaporates. Both halves are supported, both are
small, and the whole evidence base is seven studies.

That number is not a footnote. A systematic search across two databases,
filtered for methodological adequacy, returned seven usable studies of what a
holiday does to health and wellbeing. Pooled, the benefit is modest and the
fade-out after returning to work is of similar size. So the well-known
observation that the holiday feeling fades soon after work resumes is
supported, at about the strength you would expect from seven studies -- which is
to say, directionally, and not as a quantity anyone should plan around.

The most valuable thing in the paper is what it says it cannot tell you. The
authors note that vacation activities and experiences have barely been studied,
so whether it matters what you actually do on a break is unknown. Active versus
passive, at home versus away, alone versus with people -- none of it has the
research behind it that the bare fact of taking leave has. That is the question
a system that knows someone's calendar would most want answered, and the honest
answer is that nobody has run it.

A boundary worth keeping clean. Being genuinely switched off from work is a
different, larger and separately evidenced literature, and this warehouse holds
it elsewhere. A holiday is a dose; being off is a state. Using the big
detachment findings as backing for the small vacation ones would be quoting the
same idea twice under two names.

What this changes in practice is the frame, not the advice. If the effect is
small and short-lived, a break is not a repair for a chronic load problem. It is
maintenance that has to recur. Telling someone they will feel better after the
holiday is a promise this evidence does not cover, and it sets up a specific
disappointment soon after they get back.
