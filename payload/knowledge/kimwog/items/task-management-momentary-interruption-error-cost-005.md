# task-management/momentary-interruption-error-cost-005

The interruption cost that shows up in a controlled experiment is not lost
time. It is lost place.

On a task where each step had its own requirement and correct performance
depended on remembering where you were in the sequence, interruptions of about
4.4 seconds tripled the rate of sequence errors immediately afterwards.
Interruptions of about 2.8 seconds -- roughly one step's worth of time --
doubled them. Errors that did not involve losing your place were unaffected,
and reaction times were hurt less than accuracy was.

Three consequences.

First, the dose is tiny. Glancing at a notification is enough. Any policy
framed around "don't take long interruptions" is aimed at the wrong variable.

Second, the cost is invisible in a time log. Three seconds does not register as
a gap; a step done twice or skipped does not register at all. A system that
measures work by elapsed time will price this cost at zero.

Third, the exposure is specific. This is a place-keeping task -- ordered steps,
each of which must happen once. It maps well onto procedures, checklists,
routines and anything with a "did I already do that" failure mode. It does not
license a general claim that all interrupted work degrades; the field evidence
in task-management/interruption-resumption-measured-004 in fact found
interrupted tasks getting finished faster, at a cost in stress rather than
output.

Where the two meet is the useful reading: interruptions cost stress and
correctness rather than throughput, and neither of those is what a completion
log records.
