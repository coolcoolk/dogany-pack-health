  a supervisor's rating (some meta-analyses mix in objective output measures),
  and supervisor ratings disagree with each other far
  more than the field's confident numbers suggest. The meta-analytic
  interrater reliability of job performance ratings is about .52
  (Viswesvaran, Ones and Schmidt 1996), which is the value most meta-analyses
  use to correct their validity estimates upward. Sackett et al. (2022) show
  that the choice between .52 and .60 is not settled and is not innocuous:
  they adopt .60 while presenting the .52 results alongside, and note that
  choosing the lower value means a 10 percent larger upward correction --
  correcting with .52 raises mean observed validity by 39 percent, with .60 by
  29 percent.

  the point of this item. First, a predictor's measured validity is capped by
  the reliability of the thing it is predicting, so no selection method can
  look better than the criterion allows. Second, when two sources report
  different validity numbers for the same predictor, the difference is often

# work-career/criterion-problem-supervisor-ratings-002

Almost every claim about what predicts job performance is really a claim
about what predicts a supervisor's rating of job performance. That distinction does a lot
of work.

When two supervisors rate the same person, they agree at about .52. That is
the pooled meta-analytic figure, and it is the number most of this literature
uses to correct its estimates upward, on the logic that a predictor cannot
look good against a noisy target.

The choice of correction constant is not settled. The 2022 recalibration of
the field adopts .60 rather than .52, reports both, and notes that using the
lower value makes the upward correction about 10 points bigger (39 percent
above the observed figure instead of 29). There is also
evidence that agreement varies with how complex the job is, with means around
.60 for some bands and .48 for others, rather than one constant for the whole
labour market.

Two things follow that are worth carrying into any conversation about hiring
or performance:

The measured usefulness of any selection method is capped by how reliably the
outcome is measured. A method cannot predict a target better than the target
can be pinned down.

When two credible sources give different numbers for the same method, check
whether they simply chose different correction constants before treating it as
a real disagreement about evidence.

The deeper implication for anyone reasoning about their own performance
review: the rating is a measurement with substantial disagreement built into
it, not a reading off an instrument.
