# work-career/burnout-intervention-organizational-edge-010

Interventions against burn-out do something. The something is small, and the
ones that change the workplace beat the ones that change the person.

A meta-analysis of 20 controlled comparisons across 19 studies, covering 1,550
physicians, found a small overall reduction in emotional exhaustion --
standardised effect -0.29, which the authors describe as roughly a 3-point
drop on the exhaustion scale beyond what happened in the control groups.

The split underneath that average is the useful part:

- Interventions directed at the organisation: -0.45
- Interventions directed at the individual: -0.18

Roughly two and a half times the point estimate, for changing the work rather
than the worker, and the authors report the difference as significant -- though
it compares different studies rather than coming from one head-to-head trial.
Effects also looked larger among more experienced physicians and in primary
care, but those differences were not statistically significant.

This lines up with how the condition is defined in the first place. The
official definition names chronic workplace stress that has not been
successfully managed as the cause. It would be odd if resilience training
outperformed workload change, and it does not.

Two limits worth stating plainly. Even the better arm is a small effect, so
neither approach is a solution. And the evidence is in physicians; extending
it to other work is a reasonable inference but it is an inference.

The practical asymmetry for an individual: personal coping practices are the
part you control and the weaker lever; workload, schedule and role clarity are
the stronger lever and usually require someone else to move. Knowing which is
which stops a person from concluding they failed at recovering.
