# spending-saving/earmarking-partitioning-field-experiment-011

Envelope budgeting is advised constantly and tested almost never. This is the
one real test found, and it is narrower and stranger than the advice.

At a construction site in rural India, 146 day labourers paid in weekly cash,
with no bank accounts, agreed to a savings scheme. Every Saturday for fifteen
weeks a social worker came and put a set amount into savings envelopes, and
recorded which of the previous weeks' envelopes were still sealed. What varied
between households, at random, was three things: whether the savings target was
high or low, whether a picture of the household's children was on the envelope,
and whether the money went into one envelope or was split equally into two.

Splitting it mattered most. Over fourteen weeks, households whose money was
divided into two envelopes had saved about 414 rupees against 241 for those with
a single envelope. The picture helped too -- 350 against 304 -- and the two
reinforced each other, with the split doing more when the picture was there.

The result worth pausing on is the target. Setting a higher savings goal did
nothing on its own. And its effect flipped depending on the structure: with the
money split into two envelopes, a higher target raised savings; with a single
envelope, a higher target LOWERED them. Asking for more without changing the
structure made people save less.

The mechanism the authors propose is that breaking into a second sealed envelope
requires a second violation, and the guilt of that second breach is what does
the work. Which is exactly why the transfer question is serious. This is
physical cash in a sealed paper envelope, held by people paid in cash with no
bank account, at the low end of the income distribution in one country. A
labelled sub-account on a phone screen is not the same object, and nothing here
shows it behaves the same way. What travels is the shape of the advice -- split
rather than merely label, make the goal concrete, and do not raise the target
without adding the structure -- not the numbers, and not the certainty.
