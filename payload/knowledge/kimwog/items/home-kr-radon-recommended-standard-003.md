# home/kr-radon-recommended-standard-003

Radon is unusual in this whole area: it is invisible, it is a confirmed lung
carcinogen, and in Korea, for a new apartment, somebody has already measured it
for you and is legally required to have posted the result.

The health framing comes from WHO's 2009 radon handbook. Radon is the second
cause of lung cancer after smoking. Most radon-induced cases occur in smokers,
because smoking and radon combine rather than simply add. WHO proposes a
reference level of 100 becquerels per cubic metre, and says that where a country
cannot reach that under its own conditions, the level it chooses should still
not exceed 300.

Korea's own recommended standard for newly built apartments sits at 148 Bq/m3.
It comes in a table with six other substances -- formaldehyde at 210, benzene at
30, toluene at 1000, ethylbenzene at 360, xylene at 700 and styrene at 300
micrograms per cubic metre -- all of them aimed at the off-gassing of a
brand-new interior.

The genuinely useful part is not any of those numbers. It is the procedure. The
builder has to measure, has to submit the result to the local authority no later
than seven days before people move in, and has to post it publicly for sixty
days: in the common areas, at the building entrances, and on the builder's own
website. Failing to post, or posting something false, carries a fine of up to
five million won.

So for a new Korean apartment, the correct move is to read the posted number.
Not to buy a consumer radon meter, not to reason from the granite under the
site, and not to assume anything from the floor you are on. The measurement
exists and it is meant to be visible to you.

Three things this item deliberately does not do.

It does not cover your home if your home is not a new apartment. Older
buildings, detached houses, and basement or semi-basement units are all outside
this disclosure regime, and the last of those is precisely where radon tends to
accumulate. For those, the WHO reference levels are all this item gives you.

It does not treat compliance as safety. Korea's 148 is looser than WHO's
proposed 100. A posted result of, say, 130 is compliant with the Korean standard
and above the level WHO suggests acting at. Both facts are true and both should
be said.

And it does not carry an exceedance rate. There are widely repeated Korean
figures for how many new apartments come in over the standard. They circulate
through news coverage of parliamentary audits, and no ministry dataset behind
them was read at source in this pass. So no percentage is quoted here.
