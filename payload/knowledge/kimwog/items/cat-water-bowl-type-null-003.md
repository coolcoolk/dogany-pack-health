# cat/water-bowl-type-null-003

Whether a cat drinks more from a fountain than from a bowl has been tested twice
under controlled conditions, and neither test found a difference.

The better study randomised sixteen healthy cats through three water sources --
a still bowl, a circulating one and a free-falling one -- for two weeks each, in
crossover. It measured not just how much they drank but urine volume, urine
concentration, and the supersaturation values associated with both common types
of stone. None of them differed by bowl type. Urine osmolality was actually
higher on the circulating bowl than on the other two, which runs against the
fountain rather than for it.

An earlier study with nine cats had already found the same shape. Slightly more
water drunk from the flowing source, not statistically significant.

The honest reading is narrower than "fountains do nothing." Sixteen cats and nine
cats cannot rule out a small effect, and neither paper claims to. What they show
is that whatever effect exists is not big enough to appear in the only two
controlled tests anyone has run. That is a much weaker statement than the
marketing claim, and a much stronger one than nothing.

The genuinely useful part appeared in both studies independently: individual cats
had preferences, and the group did not. Three of the sixteen in the newer study
preferred a particular bowl. The older study reported the same thing in different
words. So the practical instruction is not "don't buy one." It is that the
general premise is unsupported, and whether this particular cat drinks more from
one is something the household can observe and the literature cannot tell them in
advance.

One limitation stays open. Both studies used individually housed cats under
controlled conditions. A cat at home competes for space, has its bowl placed
somewhere specific, and lives with variables neither study touched. That might
hide a real effect that only shows up in a house, or it might mean the laboratory
answer is the clean one. Nothing in either paper decides it, and no home-based
controlled test turned up.

Everything else that gets recommended alongside a fountain -- bowl material, bowl
width so the whiskers do not touch, keeping water away from the food, changing it
more often -- was not measured by either of these studies, and a search for
controlled feline tests of any of it found nothing worth citing. None of it is
asserted here in either direction.
