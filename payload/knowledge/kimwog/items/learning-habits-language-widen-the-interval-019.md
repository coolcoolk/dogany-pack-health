# learning-habits/language-widen-the-interval-019

The strongest evidence anyone has about spacing and language is also the
strangest: four psychologists spent nine years running the experiment on
themselves. Each learned three hundred foreign word pairs, split across six
conditions crossing the number of retraining sessions (thirteen or twenty-six)
with the interval between them (fourteen, twenty-eight or fifty-six days), then
tested themselves one, two, three and five years after training stopped.

The headline is a straight trade of sessions for patience. Thirteen sessions
spaced fifty-six days apart produced retention comparable to twenty-six sessions
spaced fourteen days apart. Half the work, same result, from nothing but waiting
longer between sittings. The direction held for every one of the four subjects,
and an earlier study with ordinary participants found the same thing at eight
years: a thirty-day interval left roughly twice the recall of a one-day
interval.

The part that makes this craft rather than trivia is the trap the paper
documents about itself. Wider intervals made acquisition slightly HARDER -- more
exposures were needed per word during training, and at the end of training the
narrow fourteen-day schedule actually looked best. The curves only cross during
the first year. So the schedule that wins over five years is the one that feels
worse while you are doing it and still looks worse the day you finish. Anyone
judging their own language practice by how it feels this month will
systematically choose the losing schedule. This is the same shape as the
warehouse's finding that the techniques which feel most productive are the ones
rated lowest for durable learning.

Practically, for a person studying a language: put vocabulary in a scheduler,
let the intervals grow into weeks and months rather than resetting them because
a session felt shaky, and expect the wide schedule to feel less satisfying than
a dense one. This lane's item on lapses applies with full force here, because
language study runs over years and nobody keeps a schedule for years.

Now the boundary, which matters more than the finding. Everything above is about
one thing: recalling the meaning of a word from a prompt. Not speaking. Not
listening under time pressure. Not grammar, not word choice, not the ability to
produce a sentence while someone is waiting for it. The studies measured
receptive word-pair recall, and this domain's evidence lane already establishes
that the whole learning-technique literature is far stronger on surface and
factual outcomes than on deep ones. So the ubiquitous promise -- that a spaced
vocabulary system will make you fluent -- is not merely unproven here; it runs
against what this warehouse already holds about the reach of these techniques.

State the split honestly when advising: spacing is the right tool for the
lexical layer, it is well supported there, and it will buy real time. What
produces production has not been established by anything in this lane, and a
person whose language practice consists only of a review queue is training one
layer and should know it.

Honest limit. Four subjects, who were the authors, self-administering over nine
years without laboratory control -- they report their own protocol departures,
unscheduled sessions, and uncontrolled exposure including travel to the target
country and films in the target language, and they discarded the affected data
rather than hiding it. That candour is why the study is usable at all. It is
still four people, and the eight-year corroboration is quasi-experimental. Treat
the direction as solid and any specific number as illustrative.
