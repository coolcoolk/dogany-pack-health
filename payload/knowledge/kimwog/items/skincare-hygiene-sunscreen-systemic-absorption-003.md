# skincare-hygiene/sunscreen-systemic-absorption-003

Sunscreen filters get into the blood. That is settled, it was measured by the US
regulator's own scientists in registered randomised trials, and it means less
than the headlines and more than the industry response.

Forty-eight healthy participants, four formulations -- lotion, aerosol spray,
non-aerosol spray, pump spray. Product applied at 2 mg per square centimetre to
75 percent of the body, once on day one and then four times a day at two-hour
intervals on days two through four. Thirty-four blood samples per person over
twenty-one days.

Every one of the six active ingredients tested exceeded 0.5 ng per mL in plasma,
and every one of them crossed that line on day one, after a single application.
The maxima:

- avobenzone: roughly 3.3 to 7.1 ng per mL depending on formulation
- octisalate: 4.6 to 5.8
- octinoxate: 5.2 to 7.9
- octocrylene: 6.6 to 7.8
- homosalate: 13.9 to 23.1
- oxybenzone: 180.1 to 258.1

Oxybenzone is not in the same range as the rest and should never be averaged
into a summary with them.

Now the sentence that decides how this is spoken. The 0.5 ng per mL figure is not
a safety limit. It is the threshold below which a regulator may WAIVE additional
nonclinical safety studies. Crossing it means the waiver is unavailable and the
studies are owed. It does not mean anyone was poisoned. Reporting that
"sunscreen chemicals exceeded the safety threshold" swaps one meaning for the
other, and that swap is the entire distance between what was measured and what
was said about it.

Two more things belong in any honest account. The dosing was maximal by design:
laboratory thickness, three quarters of the body, four days. That is not how
anyone uses sunscreen, and the gap between test thickness and real application is
its own item. And these were pharmacokinetic studies. They measured plasma
concentration and logged adverse events -- rash was the most common, in 14 of 48
people in the 2020 trial -- and nothing more. No endocrine, reproductive or
longer-term clinical outcome was assessed, because none was designed for.

The authors closed their own paper with the point an assistant should repeat:
these findings do not indicate that individuals should refrain from using
sunscreen. The absorption is real, the harm question is open, and the benefit
side is the one part of this domain with a randomised trial behind it.
