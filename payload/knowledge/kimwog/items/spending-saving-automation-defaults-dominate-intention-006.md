  decision, and the gap is not a matter of degree. Chetty et al. (2014) use 41
  million observations covering the population of Denmark and find that
  retirement-savings policies which rely on the individual taking an action --
  tax subsidies -- primarily induce people to SHIFT assets from taxable to
  retirement accounts rather than to save more: each USD 1 of government
  expenditure on subsidies increases total saving by only about 1 cent. Policies
  that raise contributions if the individual does nothing -- automatic employer
  contributions -- increase wealth accumulation substantially. They estimate
  that roughly 15 percent of individuals are "active savers" who respond to
  subsidies mainly by shifting assets between accounts, and 85 percent are
  "passive savers" who are unresponsive to subsidies but are heavily influenced
  by automatic contributions made on their behalf; active savers tend to be

# spending-saving/automation-defaults-dominate-intention-006

The largest measured result in household saving is not about willpower. It is
about who has to do something.

Using administrative records for the entire population of Denmark -- 41 million
observations -- researchers separated policies that require the saver to act
from policies that work if the saver does nothing. Tax subsidies, which require
action, mostly moved money that was already being saved from one account to
another: every dollar of government spending on them raised total saving by
about a penny. Automatic contributions, which require nothing, raised wealth
substantially. About 85 percent of people were passive savers, unresponsive to
incentives but strongly moved by contributions made on their behalf. The 15
percent who did respond to incentives were the wealthier and more financially
sophisticated ones -- that is, the people who needed the help least.

The default side of the same phenomenon shows up in employer plans. Switching a
retirement plan to automatic enrolment raised participation sharply, and a large
share of the automatically enrolled stayed on both the default contribution rate
and the default fund, even though almost nobody who had chosen freely had picked
that combination. The default was not a starting point; it was the answer.

A third approach commits people in advance to save part of future pay rises,
so the increase never arrives as spendable money. Of those offered it, 78
percent joined, 80 percent were still in it four pay rises later, and their
average saving rate went from 3.5 percent to 13.6 percent over about three and a
half years. That one is a non-randomised implementation at three firms with
self-selected participants, so it illustrates the mechanism rather than
measuring it.

The operational reading is blunt. Encouraging someone to save is aimed at the
15 percent who least need it. Making the transfer happen without a decision --
and best of all, timing it so the money is never seen as available -- is what
the evidence actually supports. The caveat that has to travel with it: all of
this was measured inside employer and national pension systems. That a
self-directed automatic transfer between two ordinary accounts carries the same
asymmetry is a reasonable inference from the mechanism, not a demonstrated
result.
