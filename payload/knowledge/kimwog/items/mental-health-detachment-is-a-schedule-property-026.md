# mental-health/detachment-is-a-schedule-property-026

The evidence lane's workload item ends on a careful, deflating note. Being
genuinely off work correlates with lower exhaustion, much lower fatigue, better
sleep and better wellbeing, across eighty-six publications and thirty-eight
thousand employees -- and almost all of it is cross-sectional, so nobody knows
which way the arrow points. Exhausted people find it harder to switch off.
People who cannot switch off get exhausted. A brutal job does both. The item
says an assistant may name an observable pattern and may not promise that
changing it will help.

That looks like a dead end for an assistant. It is not, because of a
decomposition worth making explicit.

Detachment as studied is a psychological state, and that half is off limits --
whether somebody was genuinely off in their head is not observable, not
measurable here, and not the assistant's business to assess.

But there is a second half, and it is structural. Whether an interval contains
no work object is a property of a calendar. It can be checked. It asserts nothing
whatsoever about a person. That is unusual in this domain and it is what makes
this one topic operationally workable when most of the domain is not.

**Measure the gap, not the mood.**

"No stretch longer than four hours without a work item in it, for eleven days
running" is a fact about a schedule. It contains no state word, no threshold
anybody invented, no causal claim. It can be wrong, and if it is, the person can
say so and point at the thing the calendar missed.

**An empty slot is not an off-interval.**

This is the operational finding, and it is the one that took actually trying it
to learn. A gap on a calendar with nothing in it is not protected time; it is
unallocated capacity, and a working week reclaims unallocated capacity by
default. Every meeting that needs to move, moves into it. Every task that
overruns, overruns into it.

So the act that does anything is not clearing a slot. It is giving the interval
an owner -- an actual thing being done, at a stated time, that has to be
displaced rather than merely filled. A booking beats a blank. Something with
another person in it beats something alone.

Note carefully what is and is not being claimed. This is a claim about how
calendars behave, which the assistant can watch directly. It is not a claim that
the resulting interval helps anybody.

**Report the count, never the consequence.**

"Three weeks with nothing off in them" is supported. "You need a break" is not,
and "this will help" is not. The evidence lane is explicit about the second one:
the correlation is real and the direction is not established, and adding the
arrow means asserting something the data does not contain.

The temptation is strong precisely because the count sounds like it implies the
conclusion. It does not. Say the count and stop -- and per the wording item, say
it once.

**The assistant is a work object.**

This is the part that gets left out of everybody's accounting, including its own.

A protected interval ends when a work-related message arrives in it. The
assistant sends work-related messages. Which means the assistant's own delivery
schedule is not a neutral observer of the quantity it is measuring -- it is part
of the quantity, and it is the only part it can change without asking anyone.

So: nothing goes out during a protected interval unless it is genuinely
time-critical, and the bar for time-critical is that acting late costs something
real. Digests batch to the edges. Notifications about the person's own workload
are the worst offenders, because a nudge about not resting is itself a piece of
work arriving in the rest. An assistant that reports the count and then interrupts
the gap it just measured has done net harm to the thing it was tracking.

**One locale note, carried rather than restated.**

The evidence lane's regional breakdown found the long-hours-and-mood association
several times larger in Asian samples than in the North American and Australian
samples that supply most of the literature -- where it was absent altogether. This
warehouse's owner is in the region where the association is largest and the
default estimates come from the region where it is not.

The correct use of that is narrow. It is a reason to keep the count visible
rather than assume an imported default describes the situation. It is not a
licence to make a claim, because the same analysis reports the divergence without
explaining it, and a bigger unexplained number is not a smaller unknown.
