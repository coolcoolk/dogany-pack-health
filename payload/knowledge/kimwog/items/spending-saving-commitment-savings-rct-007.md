reasoning: >
  Graded at the strong empirical band: it is a genuine randomised field
  experiment with random assignment of the OFFER, which is the right design for
  the question, and the effect is large and measured against a real bank
  balance rather than a self-report. It stops below the top band on external
  validity and on outcome scope. The population is one rural Philippine bank's
  existing clients; the outcome is balances at that bank, so money relocated
  from elsewhere would count as an increase. This item is shipped as the
  evidence-backed core of what "commitment device" means, because the phrase
  circulates far beyond anything that has been tested. Two things an agent
  should take from it and not more. First, restricting access works, and works
  through a mechanism the users themselves anticipate -- the people who took it
  up were the ones whose own answers said they wanted to be constrained. Second,
  most people said no, which means a commitment device is something to OFFER on
  evidence of demand, not something to prescribe. Anyone quoting the 81 point
  figure for a Korean household is quoting a Philippine rural-banking result
  outside its population.

# spending-saving/commitment-savings-rct-007

The idea of deliberately locking up your own money has one strong randomised
test behind it, and both halves of the result matter.

A Philippine bank offered a savings account that restricted withdrawals until
the saver hit a date or an amount they had chosen themselves. Which clients got
the offer was randomised. Of 710 people offered it, 202 took it -- 28.4 percent.
Twelve months later, average savings balances among those assigned to be offered
the product were 81 percentage points higher than among those assigned to
control, and the authors argue the change was durable rather than a novelty
response.

The most interesting detail is who accepted. At baseline, before anything was
offered, participants answered hypothetical questions about trading off money
now against money later. Women whose answers indicated a preference for being
committed were significantly more likely to open the restricted account. People
knew, in advance and in the abstract, that they would want to be stopped.

The other half is the take-up rate itself. Nearly three quarters of the people
offered a commitment device turned it down. That is not a failure of the
product; it is the finding that commitment is a preference some people have and
others do not. An agent that prescribes locked savings to everyone is applying a
result that only 28 percent of its own sample wanted.

Two boundaries travel with this. The population is existing clients of one rural
bank in the Philippines, which is why this item is marked to that jurisdiction
rather than as a general fact. And the outcome is the balance at the offering
bank, so it does not rule out money having been moved in from elsewhere -- the
question that the Danish population-scale work answers for automatic
contributions is left open here.
