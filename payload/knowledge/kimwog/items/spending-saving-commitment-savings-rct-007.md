# spending-saving/commitment-savings-rct-007

The idea of deliberately locking up your own money has one randomised test
behind it, and both halves of the result matter.

A Philippine bank offered a savings account that restricted withdrawals until
the saver hit a date or an amount they had chosen themselves. Which clients got
the offer was randomised. Of 710 people offered it, 202 took it -- 28.4 percent.
Twelve months later, the average change in savings balances among those assigned
to be offered the product was 81 percentage points of baseline savings higher
than among those assigned to control -- about 411 pesos, roughly US$8, on a
baseline near 503 pesos -- and the authors argue the change was durable rather
than a novelty response. That mean is imprecise (significant only at the 10
percent level) and does not clearly beat a plain marketing visit; the firmer
result is that offered clients were about 10 percentage points more likely to
have increased their savings at all.

The most interesting detail is who accepted. At baseline, before anything was
offered, participants answered hypothetical questions about trading off money
now against money later. Women whose answers indicated a preference for being
committed were more likely to open the restricted account (15.8 points more
likely; for men the difference was 4.6 points and not significant). Among
women, at least, people seem to have known in advance and in the abstract that
they would want to be stopped. But the extra savings were not significantly
larger for them than for others, and the gain was concentrated among clients who
had been inactive savers.

The other half is the take-up rate itself. Nearly three quarters of the people
offered a commitment device turned it down. That is not a failure of the
product; it is the finding that commitment is a preference some people have and
others do not. An agent that prescribes locked savings to everyone is applying a
result that only 28 percent of its own sample wanted.

Two boundaries travel with this. The population is existing clients of one rural
bank in the Philippines, which is why this item is marked to that jurisdiction
rather than as a general fact. And the outcome is the balance at the offering
bank. The authors checked for shifting between the bank's own accounts and found
none, but money moved in from other institutions or cash is not observed -- the
question that the Danish population-scale work answers for automatic
contributions is left open here. They also say the welfare effect is ambiguous:
a bigger balance is not the same as being better off when the money is locked.
