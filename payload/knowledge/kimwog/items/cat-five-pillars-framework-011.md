# cat/five-pillars-framework-011

There is a real published framework for what a cat needs from its environment,
which is more than most cat advice has behind it. There is no trial showing that
following it works, which is less than the way it usually gets cited.

Two veterinary professional bodies organised feline environmental requirements
into five pillars. A safe place. Multiple and separated key resources -- food,
water, toileting, scratching, play, and places to rest. Opportunity for play and
predatory behaviour. Positive, consistent and predictable interaction with
people. And an environment that respects how much of a cat's world is scent.

Having that structure is worth more than any single recommendation inside it. But
it is a consensus document, not an experimental result. Nobody has run a
controlled test of the framework, and the peer-reviewed review that lists the
pillars says openly that some of its recommendations come from the author's
twenty years of referral practice rather than from research.

The closest thing to evidence is a study of forty-six indoor cats with idiopathic
cystitis whose owners were given environmental-modification advice alongside
normal care. Over ten months, owners reported significant reductions in urinary
signs, in fearfulness, in nervousness. It sounds strong until you notice what is
missing: there was no control group, the owners were not blinded, the outcomes
were their own reports, and the condition being treated relapses and remits on
its own. That is the textbook setup for things looking better by themselves. The
authors call it promising adjunctive therapy, which is the honest word for it.

What this framework does do is settle the domain's largest piece of folklore.
"Cats are low maintenance" is how the species is usually described, and the
professional position is the opposite: meeting environmental needs is stated as
essential rather than optional, and the guidelines exist because unmet needs turn
up as medical and behavioural disease. The difference between a cat and a dog is
not that the cat needs less. It is that a cat's needs are quieter, and a species
whose distress is hard to read gets mistaken for a species without any.

One detail gets lost in retelling. The second pillar is not "put out enough
bowls." The operative words are multiple and separated, and the list of resources
includes places to scratch, play and sleep. In a household with more than one
cat, separation is the part that carries the meaning.

Finally, there are no numbers in this framework. No amount of play, no count of
resting places, no scent protocol. Every specific figure that circulates attached
to it came from somewhere other than a measurement.
