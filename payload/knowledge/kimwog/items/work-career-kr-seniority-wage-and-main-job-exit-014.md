# work-career/kr-seniority-wage-and-main-job-exit-014

Almost everything in the rest of this domain was measured in labour markets
where pay is not tied to how long you have been there. Korea is not one of
those, so the transfer has to be checked.

What the OECD country review records:

Pay tracks tenure hard. In manufacturing, someone with 20 to 30 years of
tenure earned on average 2.7 times what someone with under a year earned -- a
ratio the OECD says is much higher than anywhere else in the OECD except
Japan.

The system is still the norm. As of 2017, a seniority wage system was in place
at 60.3 percent of establishments with 100 or more employees. The OECD calls
it perhaps the single biggest driver of poor employment outcomes for older
workers in Korea.

Long tenure at older ages is rare, not common. In 2017 only 16.3 percent of
workers aged 60 to 64, and 9.4 percent of those 65 and over, had been in their
job ten years or more. The OECD averages were 49.8 percent and 58.2 percent.
Workers aged 55 to 64 have shorter tenure in Korea than in any other OECD
country with comparable data.

Tenure rises steeply with firm size: 2.5 years in firms under 5 employees,
3.1 years at 5 to 9, 10.4 years at 300 or more.

And the piece that connects to the other half of this domain. Most people
leaving their main job start what the review calls a second career, which
typically means a sharp fall in earnings, job quality and security -- and for
many it means self-employment or running a business, in the review's own
words, without necessarily having the skills or networks necessary for
success.

That is worth sitting with. In the Korean case, independent work is very
often a displacement outcome rather than a chosen bet, which changes what the
survival statistics in this domain mean when someone reads them.

One firm caution on using this item: the numbers are from 2012 to 2017 and the
institutions have been under active reform since. Store the structure, re-check
the percentages.
