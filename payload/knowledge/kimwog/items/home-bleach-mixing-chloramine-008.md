# home/bleach-mixing-chloramine-008

This is the one item in this domain where the correct response to uncertainty is
not to hedge.

Household bleach and household ammonia react to form chloramine compounds. A
1986 report from a single centre describes three cases in one year of
life-threatening toxic pneumonitis from that exposure. Every one of the three
had a prolonged hospital stay and was left with symptomatic residual changes on
imaging. The authors' concluding note is that this may be a frequent cause of
acute pneumonitis that is not well recognised.

Three patients is a small number. It is also the only ethical study design
available for this question, and severity is exactly what a case series is good
at establishing. Nobody is going to run a trial.

The reason this matters domestically rather than industrially is that the
exposure is completely ordinary. Two products under the sink. A bathroom with
the door shut. A surface still wet from the first thing you sprayed. That is the
whole scenario.

That household chemical exposure is a high-volume everyday event, not an exotic
accident, shows up in surveillance: US poison centres logged 45,550 cleaner and
disinfectant exposure calls in the first quarter of 2020 alone, up around twenty
percent on the year before. A national survey in the same period found 39
percent of respondents doing something explicitly non-recommended with cleaning
products. Neither of those numbers is a count of mixing injuries and neither
should be quoted as one -- they establish that this is common territory, not
that mixing caused them.

The rule that follows is deliberately absolute: do not combine bleach with any
other cleaning product. One product at a time. Rinse. Ventilate.

Why absolute rather than specific to ammonia? Because you often cannot tell. A
lot of glass and general-purpose cleaners are ammonia-based, and nobody reads
the ingredient panel with wet gloves on. A rule with a lookup step in it fails
at exactly the moment it is needed. A rule with no lookup step has no failure
mode.

And note that sequential use is still mixing. Spraying a second product onto a
surface still wet with the first, or emptying two products into the same bucket
or the same drain, is the same reaction in a different container.

One thing deliberately not carried here: any concentration or exposure-time
number. No source in this sprint gives one that applies to a household room, and
supplying a threshold would quietly convert a safety instruction into a
permission slip.
