# skincare-hygiene/sunscreen-rct-what-was-shown-001

Sunscreen is the one thing in this domain that has been put through a randomised
controlled trial with real endpoints. That trial exists, it is thirty years old,
it happened in one Queensland town, and its results are both genuine and
narrower than the version that circulates.

Nambour, Australia, latitude 26 degrees south. 1,621 residents were randomised
into four groups in a factorial design: daily sunscreen to head, neck, arms and
hands or sunscreen at their own discretion, crossed with 30 mg per day of oral
beta-carotene or placebo. Four and a half years.

What it found on cancer:

- Counting PEOPLE with a first new skin cancer, there was no significant
  difference between the sunscreen arms for either tumour type. Basal-cell
  carcinoma 2,588 versus 2,509 per 100,000, rate ratio 1.03. Squamous-cell
  carcinoma 876 versus 996 per 100,000, rate ratio 0.88. Both intervals cross
  one.
- Counting TUMOURS, squamous-cell carcinoma was significantly lower in the daily
  sunscreen group: 1,115 versus 1,832 per 100,000, rate ratio 0.61 with an
  interval of 0.46 to 0.81. Basal-cell carcinoma was still null.

So the famous "sunscreen prevents skin cancer" line rests on a tumour-count
reduction in one of the two common skin cancers, and not on fewer people getting
cancer. Say it that way.

Ten years after the trial stopped, the melanoma follow-up found 11 new primaries
in the daily group against 22 in the discretionary group. Hazard ratio 0.50, but
the interval runs 0.24 to 1.02 and the p value is 0.051 -- it does not exclude no
effect. The invasive subset does exclude it, at 3 events against 11. Three
events. That number belongs in every sentence that quotes the hazard ratio.

The cleanest result in the trial is the one nobody markets. Among the 903
participants under 55, assessors blinded to allocation graded skin
microtopography before and after. The daily sunscreen group showed no detectable
increase in skin aging across four and a half years, and 24 percent less aging
than the discretionary group, relative odds 0.76 with an interval of 0.59 to
0.98. The authors named their own limits: missing outcome data, and modest power
to detect moderate effects.

And the control case, which is the most useful thing in the trial for a domain
this commercially captured: 30 mg per day of oral beta-carotene was randomised
in the same population, by the same investigators, for the same outcomes. It did
nothing. Not to skin cancer, not to skin aging. An oral supplement sold for the
skin was put beside a topical measure under identical conditions, and only one of
them moved.

Two limits on carrying any of this forward. It is one trial in one town at one
latitude in a population with extreme sun exposure, and its three publications
are follow-ups of that same cohort, not independent replications. And the
sunscreen was applied under trial conditions. What ordinary people actually put
on their skin is a different quantity, which is its own item.
