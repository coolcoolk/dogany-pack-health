# work-career/pay-transparency-equilibrium-006

Making pay visible can lower average pay. That is not a cynical reading, it
is the measured result for one kind of transparency -- US laws protecting
coworkers' right to discuss salary -- and the reason is on the employer's side
of the table.

The argument runs like this. When pay is secret, an employer can quietly
overpay one person to close one deal. When pay is visible, that same raise
now sets a precedent that has to be paid to everyone comparable, or defended
to everyone comparable. So the employer can credibly refuse -- and both sides
know it. The individual's leverage falls.

Tested against United States state laws protecting workers' right to discuss
salary with each other, wages declined by roughly 2 percent after the laws
took effect. And consistent with the model, the decline was smallest where
workers had little individual leverage to start with, since there was less to
take away.

The distinction that most commentary drops: this is about the LEVEL of pay,
not its SPREAD. Transparency can narrow the gap between people while lowering
the middle. Someone citing this literature to argue for or against
transparency is usually quoting only the half that suits them.

What it changes for one person negotiating: in a transparent regime, you are
not asking your employer to pay you more. You are asking them to move a band.
That is a different, harder request, and it usually has to be argued at the
level of the band rather than the individual.
