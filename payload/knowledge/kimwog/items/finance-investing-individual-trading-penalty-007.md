# finance-investing/individual-trading-penalty-007

The classic study of self-directed retail accounts found that the households
who traded the most earned 11.4 percent a year while the market returned 17.9
percent over the same window. The stocks they chose were not unusually bad.
The gap is what the trading itself cost them.

Two things must travel with those numbers. First, the sample is one large US
discount broker over 1991-1996, when commissions and spreads were far higher
than they are now, so the size of the penalty is not transferable to a
present-day account. Second, the mechanism is transferable: activity is a
cost, and it is a cost the investor fully controls.

The usable form of this is not a percentage. It is a direction: when in
doubt about whether to act on a portfolio, the base rate favours acting less
often. Do not extend this item into claims about how far the average investor
underperforms the funds they own -- that is a different and much weaker
literature, and this lane does not carry it.
