# learning-habits/writing-volume-measurement-trap-020

Writing practice is the one part of self-directed study that comes with a
famous rule, a famous number, and almost no evidence. The rule is "write every
day". The number is some version of "the daily writers produced nine times as
many pages". Both fail on contact with the original papers.

A 2016 article in the academic-development literature went back and read them.
The study that launched the mantra worked with ten hand-picked chronic
procrastinators, identified from a larger group at the author's own university,
whom the author personally mentored and monitored at least twice a week for a
year. The nine-times-as-many-pages figure that everyone quotes does not appear
in that article at all, except obliquely in a diagram that is hard to read; and
the "accountability partners" the productive group reported to turned out to be
the researcher himself. The companion study had twenty-seven finishers, nine per
group, all self-identified procrastinators from campuses in one American city,
with fifteen of the original forty-two dropping out along the way.

Then there is the design detail that should end the discussion. That study's
control group was instructed to abstain from all non-essential academic writing
for ten weeks. Of course it produced almost nothing. The comparison measures
whether people followed an instruction not to write; it does not measure what a
writing habit does. And the outputs being compared were page counts that may
have been longhand drafts rather than manuscript pages.

Neither study has been replicated. Neither shows the effect outlasting the
intervention; the author himself reports participants sliding back once his
personal sessions stopped. And when the critic surveyed 1323 academics in
fifteen countries, including a set of writers peer-nominated as exemplary, only
about one in eight reported writing every weekday. Seven in eight successful
academic writers do not do the thing the rule says is required.

The craft lesson is not "don't write regularly". Regular writing may well be a
fine practice, and the critic says so herself while dismantling the mantra. The
lesson is about the measurement. Page count is what the instrument produces
cheaply, so page count became the outcome, and once it is the outcome you can
manufacture an impressive contrast by telling the comparison group to stop
writing. This is the writing equivalent of counting sets without checking
whether the reps happened. Any practice ledger that scores a writing session by
words produced will reward the sessions that generate text and punish the ones
that reorganise an argument, cut a section, or establish that a chapter does not
work -- and will do it invisibly, because the number goes down either way.

Two consequences for how a study practice should treat writing. Do not encode a
universal daily cadence as a rule, and do not present one as evidence-backed;
what the survey evidence supports is variety, not a schedule. And when
measuring, record the session, not the output volume -- or if volume is
recorded, never let it be the thing the person is scored on.

Honest limit. The strong part of this item is the refusal, which rests on a
published critique that went to the primary sources and reported what was in
them. The alternative is weak: a cross-sectional self-report survey of a partly
self-selected sample can show that successful writers vary, and cannot establish
what anyone should do instead. Nothing in this lane knows the right writing
cadence. It knows that the famous one is unfounded and that the number attached
to it is not in the paper.
