# skincare-hygiene/kr-expert-endorsement-prohibition-014

The most common trust shortcut in skincare marketing is a doctor. Under Korean
law that shortcut is not merely weak evidence. It is prohibited.

The advertising annex to the Cosmetics Act enforcement rule states that a seller
must not make, or imply, any claim that a physician, dentist, doctor of Korean
medicine, pharmacist, medical institution or other medical or pharmaceutical
expert designates, endorses, recommends, guides, researches, develops or uses the
cosmetic.

There is one exception and it is narrow enough to be a test in itself. Where a
human-application test result conforming to the statutory definition of a
cosmetic has been publicly recognised through a learned-society presentation or
equivalent, the literature may be cited within that scope -- provided the citation
conveys the original meaning of the work accurately, and provided the researcher's
name, the publication name and the date of publication are all clearly stated.
An advertisement gesturing at "clinical studies" without those three identifiers
is not using the exception. It is simply breaking the rule.

The same annex also bars absolute superlatives such as best or finest in
comparative advertising, bars claims that are literally or partly true but likely
to leave consumers with a false overall impression, and bars advertising any
quality or efficacy that cannot be or has not been objectively verified.

What a compliant efficacy claim must rest on is set by a separate notice. It
accepts four kinds of substantiation: human-application test data, in-vitro test
data, equivalent or better investigation data such as papers and academic
literature, and survey results. The substantiation must relate directly to the
claim actually made. Human-application tests must be supervised by a relevant
specialist or by someone with five or more years of experience in cosmetic human
testing, must follow Helsinki-declaration ethics, and must obtain written
informed consent. Surveys must be run by a body independent of the business, with
representative sampling, and with respondents kept unaware of the survey's
purpose.

Two things follow from that for anyone reading a Korean claim.

A legal claim is not necessarily a trial-backed claim. A properly conducted
consumer survey is an accepted substantiation route.

And the regulator's own worked examples of what does NOT substantiate a claim are
unusually blunt, and worth carrying verbatim in spirit: a consumer or user-
experience survey does not substantiate an efficacy claim, and a patent for an
acne-improving cosmetic composition does not substantiate that the product
improves acne. A patent is a description of an invention, not evidence that it
works.

One phrase this item deliberately does not cover. Dermatologist tested. The
prohibition addresses endorsement, recommendation and use by a professional. A
bare claim of having been tested was not located as a defined term in any
regulation read in this pass, and no standard specifying what such a test must
consist of was found. Treat it as an undefined marketing phrase, not as a
regulated one.
