# learning-habits/session-stop-condition-021

A training session ends for a reason. The sets are done, or the last set failed,
or the planned work is complete. A study session usually just stops, and because
that feels unsatisfying people import a stopping rule from productivity culture
and attach a number to it.

Both circulating numbers are undrivable.

Twenty-five minutes is the setting its inventor personally landed on, having
tried a range of durations and picked the one that suited him. That is a
perfectly honest origin story and it is not evidence. No comparison of study
session lengths against learning outcomes was located.

Ninety minutes is worse. The claim borrows a construct from sleep research about
a cycle of rest and activity, and asserts that it dictates how long a person can
study. Every retrievable source applying it to study-session length was a
productivity blog or the marketing of a timer product. Not one primary testing
it as a study-session length was found. It is recorded here as refused, so that
its ubiquity is never mistaken for support.

What is available instead is a stop condition that at least has a derivation.
The day's queue of due items is finished, and the session ends. That is
non-arbitrary in a specific and limited sense: the size of the queue was not
picked out of the air. It came from the daily limits; the limits were sized
against the intake rate; the intake rate commits a load roughly ten times its
size; and the intervals underneath all of it were chosen against a retention
horizon that is an actual decision about what you want to still know and when.
Every link in that chain is either documented practice or arithmetic. A timer's
number connects to nothing.

There is a second reason to prefer finishing work over finishing time, and it is
reasoning rather than a finding. A session running against a clock has no
particular reason to keep the reps honest as the clock runs down, and the
cheapest way to feel productive under time pressure is to stop attempting and
start reading answers -- which is exactly the condition that performed worse at
equal time in this lane's rep item. A queue does not offer that shortcut,
because the queue does not shrink for exposures.

Practically: end the session when the day's due work is done. If the due work is
routinely larger than the day can hold, that is not a session-length problem and
no timer will fix it -- it is the intake problem, and it is fixed upstream. If a
time box is useful for getting the session to start at all, use it, and be clear
about what it is: a calendar device, with no claim about learning attached.

Honest limit, and it is the whole item. Nothing here is measured. There is no
trial on session length, none on stop conditions, and none comparing
queue-empty against timed study. What this item ships is one structural argument
and two refusals, and the refusals are the more valuable half.
