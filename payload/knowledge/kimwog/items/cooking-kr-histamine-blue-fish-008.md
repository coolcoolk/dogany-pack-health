# cooking/kr-histamine-blue-fish-008

Blue-backed fish are the case where cooking longer is the wrong instinct. Their
red muscle is rich in histidine, and when the fish sits warm, bacterial enzymes
convert it to histamine. Korea's food-safety authority states the consequence
without hedging: once histamine has formed, heating does not destroy it. It also
states how little abuse it takes -- a single day at room temperature can produce
enough to make someone ill.

The illness is scombroid poisoning: flushing, hives, vomiting, diarrhoea,
dizziness, usually soon after eating and often mistaken for an allergy.

The regulatory number announced in 2012 is 200 mg/kg or less, applied to simply
processed forms of mackerel, tuna, salmon, saury, herring, anchovy, Spanish
mackerel and sardine -- frozen, salted, canned, dried, or cut into flesh and
fillets. The household advice issued alongside it: eat refrigerated fish within
about a week, keep frozen fish for two weeks or more, thaw in the refrigerator
rather than on the counter, cook it straight after thawing, never refreeze it
once thawed, and do not leave salted mackerel sitting at room temperature.

For a live cooking session, only one line of this is a decision rule, and it is
the first one. Fish that has been out is a discard question, not a cooking
question. There is no temperature and no duration that undoes it.

Two things are deliberately weaker than they look. The 200 mg/kg figure and the
species list come from a 2012 announcement and were not re-checked against the
Food Code in force now -- this warehouse has already been caught once quoting a
superseded Korean standard, so the figure is recorded rather than certified. And
the usual second source for the heat-stability point, the US fishery-hazards
guidance, would not load in this pass, so nothing was taken from it. The detail
a fuller item would carry, about the histamine-forming enzyme surviving freezing
and restarting on thaw, is therefore simply absent here rather than asserted.
