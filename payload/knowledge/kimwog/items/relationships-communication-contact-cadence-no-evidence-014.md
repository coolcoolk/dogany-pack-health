# relationships-communication/contact-cadence-no-evidence-014

How often should you be in touch with someone? Nobody has published an answer.

A targeted search for any primary study prescribing a contact frequency, an
interval, or a minimum number of contacts for keeping a relationship healthy
returned nothing. The health literature this kind of rule usually leans on does
not contain one either: its exposures are composite social-integration indices,
single-item loneliness scales, and whether a person lives alone. Not contact
counts. Not intervals.

Be careful about what that licenses. It licenses refusing to state a cadence as
evidence-based. It does not license the opposite claim -- nobody has shown that
contact frequency is irrelevant, because nobody has run that study either.

Two things are close enough to a rule that they get mistaken for one.

The first is the "fifty hours to make a casual friend, two hundred hours to make
a good friend" figures. That study is real. What it did was ask people --
recruited online, recently relocated -- to estimate from memory how many hours
they had spent with a new acquaintance. Those are recalled estimates producing
descriptive averages. No hours were measured, none were assigned, and no
threshold was tested against any alternative. The study exists; the rule of thumb
was built on top of it by retelling.

The second is the relationship-count folklore -- a hundred and fifty contacts
arranged in neat layers of five, fifteen and fifty, each with its own
prescribed contact rhythm. The count itself cannot be derived from the analysis
it comes from; the confidence intervals run from four to five hundred and twenty.
Layers that are not statistically supportable cannot generate a per-layer
schedule, and the schedules themselves have no source at all.

What is actually supported is narrower and differently shaped. The evidence
speaks to the single act, not the interval: people underestimate how welcome one
reach-out is, and they underestimate it most for the people they are least close
to. That is a nudge at the moment of hesitation, not a calendar.

So for anything holding contact records: if the user sets an interval, that is
their preference and it is perfectly legitimate on those terms. It simply must
never be described as what the research recommends. And if a number is going to
appear at all, it should arrive with its provenance attached, because the real
risk here is not a wrong number -- it is a fluent invented one sitting beside
sourced material and borrowing its credibility.

What would close this gap: a study that actually manipulated contact frequency
and measured relationship quality, or longitudinal data with objectively logged
contact rather than recalled contact. Neither exists yet, and the second
requirement is not pedantry -- self-reported social data in this domain is known
to be biased in a consistent direction.
