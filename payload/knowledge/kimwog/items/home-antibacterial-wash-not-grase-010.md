# home/antibacterial-wash-not-grase-010

The antibacterial hand wash question was not settled by a study. It was settled
by a regulator running out of patience.

The US FDA's 2016 final rule found nineteen active ingredients -- triclosan and
triclocarban the best known -- not generally recognised as safe and effective
for over-the-counter consumer antiseptic washes. It gave two independent
reasons. The data submitted were insufficient to show any additional benefit
compared with non-antibacterial soap and water. And the available information
was insufficient to establish the safety of long-term, daily, repeated exposure.

The structure of that finding is what makes it strong. This was not one
experiment coming out null. It was a rulemaking with an open docket in which the
manufacturers held the burden of demonstrating that their products were better
than plain soap, had every commercial incentive to do so, and did not.

The scope is where almost everyone gets this wrong, so it travels with the
claim. The rule covers rinse-off consumer washes. It expressly excludes
alcohol-based hand sanitisers, health-care antiseptics, first-aid antiseptics,
and food-industry antiseptics. If someone cites this rule as evidence against
hand sanitiser, they are citing the wrong document.

Two more boundaries.

This is not a finding that the products are dangerous. It is a finding that
their claimed advantage was never demonstrated and their long-term safety was
never established. Those are different statements from harm.

And it is a US determination. The evidentiary situation is not
jurisdiction-bound, but the ruling is, and no equivalent Korean determination
was read at source here. That is why this item is filed under a US locale rather
than as universal -- the same discipline that kept the poultry temperature item
on hold until Korea's own food-safety authority had been checked directly.

The practical version is short. Plain soap and water is the comparator the
antibacterial version failed to beat. Use the comparator.
