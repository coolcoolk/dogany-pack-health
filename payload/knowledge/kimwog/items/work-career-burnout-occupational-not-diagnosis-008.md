# work-career/burnout-occupational-not-diagnosis-008

Burn-out is officially an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition.
That is the World Health Organization's own wording, and it changes how the
word should be used.

It carries the code QD85 in ICD-11, and it sits in the chapter covering
reasons people contact health services that are not illnesses.

The definition, as written:

Burn-out is a syndrome conceptualized as resulting from chronic workplace
stress that has not been successfully managed. It is characterized by three
dimensions: feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion; increased mental
distance from one's job, or feelings of negativism or cynicism related to
one's job; and reduced professional efficacy. Burn-out refers specifically to
phenomena in the occupational context and should not be applied to describe
experiences in other areas of life.

Three things follow.

There is no clinical threshold to cross. Asking whether you are burned out is
not a question a diagnosis can answer, and anyone offering a cutoff score is
offering a research instrument's convention, not a medical finding.

The scope restriction is part of the definition. Burn-out is about work. The
WHO explicitly says not to use it for exhaustion in the rest of life. That is
a real constraint on the word, not pedantry.

The cause named in the definition is chronic workplace stress that has not
been successfully managed. That phrasing puts the workplace in the frame,
which matters when the only remedies on offer are personal ones.

If what is being described is persistent low mood, loss of interest across
life rather than at work, or anything with a clinical shape, that is a
different question and belongs with a clinician, not with this word.
