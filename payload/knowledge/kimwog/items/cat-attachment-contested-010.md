# cat/attachment-contested-010

Whether a cat is attached to its person, in the sense the word carries in
developmental psychology, has been studied twice with borrowed methodology and
answered both ways.

One study, in 2019, reported that cats show distinct attachment styles toward
their caregivers, judged against criteria developed for human infants, and argued
that whatever mechanism explains cross-species attachment is not specific to
dogs. An earlier study, in 2015, put twenty cat-owner pairs through an adapted
version of the same test and reached the opposite conclusion: cats vocalised more
when their owner left them than when a stranger did, but nothing else in the
behaviour matched secure attachment, and the authors described cats as typically
autonomous rather than looking to a person for security.

Something is deliberately missing from that paragraph. A specific percentage of
cats classified as securely attached is quoted almost everywhere the 2019 study
is discussed. It is not quoted here. The full text could not be retrieved in this
pass, from the publisher or from the open-access mirrors, and the record that was
available contains neither a sample size nor a percentage. The figure may be
exactly right. It was not verified, and a number that travels only through
retellings does not get repeated here just because the finding is one people
would like to be true.

Both studies are small. The 2015 one used ten pairs per test version. And both
inherit the same doubt, which the 2015 authors raise themselves: the Strange
Situation was designed for human infants, and it assumes that separation in an
unfamiliar room is what causes the stress. If a cat is stressed by the unfamiliar
room regardless of who is in it, the reading is confounded, and that objection
applies just as much to the study whose answer is more appealing.

The direction of the disagreement is easy to get backwards, so it is worth saying
plainly. Neither of these studies supports the idea that cats are indifferent.
The disagreement is about what kind of bond exists and whether the infant
framework is the right instrument for describing it -- not about whether there is
one. Citing the 2015 result as evidence that cats do not care inverts what it
found.

And nothing here says anything about a particular cat. These are protocols with
defined procedures run in a controlled setting. A cat that follows someone
around, or does not come to the door, has not been measured.
