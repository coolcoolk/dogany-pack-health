# finance-investing/return-skewness-diversification-003

Most individual stocks lose to cash. That is not a slogan, it is what the
full population of listed stocks looks like when you measure lifetime
compound returns: over 1926-2016 in the US, the majority of individual stocks
returned less than one-month Treasury bills over their listed lifetimes, and
essentially all of the net wealth the stock market created above Treasury
bills came from about four percent of the firms.

The same shape shows up globally. Across roughly 64,000 stocks worldwide from
1990 to 2020, 55.2 percent of US stocks and 57.4 percent of non-US stocks
underperformed one-month US Treasury bills. Outside the US the picture is
marginally worse, not better.

The practical consequence is a structural one rather than a taste for safety.
A market rises because a handful of extreme winners drag the average up. If
a portfolio holds only a few names, the most likely outcome is that it does
not hold any of those winners, and it therefore underperforms the index it
was picked from -- not because of bad luck but because that is what the
distribution looks like. Diversification is how a portfolio guarantees it
owns the tail.
