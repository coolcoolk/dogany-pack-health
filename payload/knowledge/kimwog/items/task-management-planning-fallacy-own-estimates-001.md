# task-management/planning-fallacy-own-estimates-001

People systematically underestimate how long their own tasks will take. In the
study that named the effect, honours-thesis students predicted on average that
they would finish in 33.9 days and actually took 55.5 days. Fewer than a third
(29.7%) finished inside their own best estimate.

The part that matters operationally is what happened when the same students
were asked for a worst case. Told to predict "if everything went as poorly as
it possibly could", they said 48.6 days -- still short of the 55.5 they
actually took, and fewer than half (48.7%) finished even by that date. Their
absolute error was no smaller when they were instructed to be pessimistic than
when they gave a best guess. Asking someone to pad an estimate shifts the
number without making it accurate.

Two things follow for an agent holding a person's own estimates:

- An estimate is good information about ORDER and poor information about
  DURATION. Predictions correlated r = .77 with actual completion times, so
  the person genuinely knows which of their tasks is the long one. They do not
  know how long it is.
- The remedy that worked in this paper was not "add a buffer". It was making
  the person connect the prediction to relevant past experience. The same paper
  found that outside observers, who naturally reason from the person's history
  rather than from their plan, OVERestimated instead. The correction is a
  change of information source, not a multiplier.

The specific day-counts come from one small sample on one kind of task; do not
carry them as a conversion factor. The structural claim -- own-estimate
optimistic, pessimism instruction ineffective, past-experience anchoring
effective -- is what transfers. The base-rate side of this is
task-management/reference-class-base-rate-002.
