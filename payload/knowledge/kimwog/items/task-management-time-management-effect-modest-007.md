# task-management/time-management-effect-modest-007

The only large synthesis of whether time management works pools 158 studies and
almost 54,000 people, and its answer is: modestly, and more for how you feel
than for what you produce.

The correlations are all in the 0.2-0.3 band. Job performance sits at about
0.26 and falls to about 0.19 after correcting for publication bias -- the
weakest of the four outcomes once corrected. Academic achievement is about the
same before correction. Overall wellbeing is higher at about 0.31, and distress
runs the other way at about -0.22.

The sharpest result is inside the wellbeing category: the correlation with LIFE
satisfaction (about 0.43) is far larger than the correlation with JOB
satisfaction (about 0.25). Time management tracks how someone's life feels much
more closely than how their work feels.

Three limits that must travel with these numbers:

- They are correlations, from mostly cross-sectional studies. Someone whose
  work is going well will also look organised. Nothing here shows that adopting
  a practice produces the outcome.
- Heterogeneity is enormous (I-squared between 74% and 93%). The pooled figure
  is a weak summary of a very mixed literature.
- "Time management" is not one treatment. The authors note that studies often
  do not say what was actually taught, so the construct being correlated is a
  loose family of self-reported habits.

For a task agent the honest framing is: helping someone run their work is worth
doing and the measured returns are real but modest, and the return is more
likely to show up in how the person feels about their life than in a
throughput number. Selling it on output overstates the strongest thing the
evidence supports.
