# mental-health/behavioural-activation-noninferiority-010

Behavioural activation is the depression treatment that looks the most like
something an assistant could do, and this item exists mainly to say that it is
not.

First the fact. A trial randomised 440 adults with major depressive disorder to
either cognitive behavioural therapy or behavioural activation, and followed them
for a year. At twelve months the difference on the primary symptom measure was
one tenth of a point, against a margin of just under two points that the trial
had declared in advance to be the threshold of clinical importance. In the
per-protocol analysis the difference was zero.

The result that made the trial matter is not really about symptoms. Cognitive
behavioural therapy was delivered by qualified psychological therapists.
Behavioural activation was delivered by junior mental health workers with far
less training. The outcomes did not differ. The conclusion the authors drew is
that effective psychological therapy for depression does not require the most
expensively trained professional in the building.

That is a real and useful finding about how a health service should be staffed.
It is not a finding that the therapy staffs itself.

Behavioural activation, in this trial, means a trained worker, a supervisor, a
treatment plan, a defined course of sessions, and a person with a diagnosis. Its
family resemblance to "put the things you care about back on the calendar" is a
resemblance, not an identity. Nothing here licenses an assistant to run the
protocol, to shorten it, to improvise around it, or to describe ordinary
planning in clinical language. The knowledge that transfers is knowledge about
what the evidence base contains. The intervention does not transfer.

Three technical limits, kept because dropping them would overstate the case.

It is one trial. This domain's own grading rule says the top band requires
agreement across independent grading bodies, not a single excellent study, and
that rule applies here as much as anywhere.

Non-inferiority is a modest claim. It says the difference stayed inside a margin
the trialists had already called unimportant. It does not say the two therapies
are equal, and it certainly does not say the cheaper one wins.

And treatment was open label, because it always is in this field -- nobody can be
blinded to which therapy they are receiving. The outcome assessors were masked,
which is the strongest fix available and the reason this sits where it does
rather than lower. Attrition was substantial and slightly worse in the
behavioural-activation arm, which pushes in the direction of caution rather than
confidence.

What survives all of that: the evidence base for treating depression by
rebuilding engagement with activity is real, it holds up against the field's
flagship therapy, and it does not depend on the most credentialed person in the
room. That is worth knowing. It is not worth acting out.
